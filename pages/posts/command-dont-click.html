Watch a top-level StarCraft match and the first thing you notice is the hands. Professional players sustain 300 or more actions per minute, and commentators quote that number the way football analysts quote a quarterback's arm strength. Somewhere along the way, a lot of people decided that this is what real-time strategy is: a dexterity test with a tech tree attached.

SplinterFaction is built on a different belief. The game is won by the player who makes better decisions about their army and their economy, and the controls exist to carry those decisions out with as few clicks as possible. Speed still helps, the way it helps in anything. It just isn't the thing being tested.

The other family tree

That belief has a pedigree. In 1997, Total Annihilation let players queue long chains of orders, leave factories building, and run an economy that flowed continuously instead of arriving in lumps. Ten years later, Supreme Commander added strategic zoom, so an entire theater of war fit on one screen and the player saw the battle the way a general would.

Both games grew fiercely competitive communities without demanding concert-pianist hands. The best players were the ones who scouted, read the map, scaled their economy at the right moment and put the right army in the right place. The interface handled the busywork so the player could handle the war.

SplinterFaction sits squarely in that lineage, alongside Earth 2150. The question it asks is never “can you keep up?” It asks “do you know what you want to do?”

Every order should mean something

APM is a poor measure of skill because so much of it is waste. Much of the clicking in a high-speed match is repeated move commands, selection cycling and reissuing orders the game has forgotten. SplinterFaction's controls are designed to cut that waste, so one deliberate action does the work of twenty frantic ones.

Tool What you do What it saves you
Custom formations Hold right-click and drag a line; your units spread along the shape you drew Nudging units one at a time into a firing line, a screen or a flank
Fire while moving Give a move order; units keep shooting and pick the most efficient targets as they go Halting the army every time you want it to fight
Repeat orders Switch repeat on and the order queue loops Reissuing the same patrol, raid route or build list all game
Line and brick building Hold Shift and drag to queue a line of structures; Shift + Alt for a solid brick Placing buildings one click at a time
Connect-the-dots EM Converters Hold right-click on a resource location and drag across the others Finding and queuing each spot separately
Upgrade buttons Click the button above an extractor or commander, with the cost shown up front Menu digging, and guessing what an upgrade will cost

Take the formation drag. In a click-driven game, setting a concave arc around a choke point takes a dozen selections and a dozen move orders, and the arc is stale before you finish. Here it is one gesture: you draw the arc and the army takes it. The skill lies in knowing where the arc belongs.

Firing on the move does the same for combat. In many RTS games an army in motion is an army that isn't shooting, so fighting well means a constant stop, shoot, move rhythm for every squad. SplinterFaction units fire as they travel and choose the most efficient targets themselves. You can reposition constantly, and your clicks go toward where the army should be.

Repeat orders do the same thing for time. An order you would otherwise reissue every thirty seconds becomes a standing instruction. Your attention goes back to the one job software can't do for you, which is deciding.

Building follows the same rule. Hold Shift and drag to lay a line of structures, or Shift + Alt to fill in a solid brick. For EM Converters, hold right-click on a resource location and sweep across the others, connect-the-dots style, and each one is queued. You don't have to be terribly precise.

One thing the game deliberately won't do is upgrade for you. Metal extractors and commanders once upgraded automatically, and it made the economy significantly harder to manage. Now each has an upgrade button above it showing the cost, so upgrading several at once takes a few quick clicks and the timing stays your call.

Macro is the real fight

Strip away the clicking and what remains is management, and management is hard. Metal comes from fixed points on the map, so every extractor is territory you must take and hold. Energy has to be built. Supply caps the size of your army, which makes every unit you field a choice against another you could have fielded.

The commander raises the stakes. Upgrading him unlocks tech tiers and stronger weapons, yet in a 1v1 he is also your loss condition: if he falls, the game is over. How far forward you bring your most powerful asset, and when you spend on him instead of the army, are calls no amount of hand speed can make for you.

The two factions carry the same idea into army handling. Loz Alliance shields regenerate quickly after combat, so a Loz player wins by choosing engagements and pulling back to recharge. Federation of Kala hulls repair themselves and their units can cloak, so a Kala player wins through mobility, timing and angles. Neither plan needs fast fingers, and both punish a player who isn't thinking.

The same lesson everywhere else

Other fields worked this out long ago.

Chess. Blitz and classical chess use the same pieces and the same rules, yet few would call blitz the deeper game. Take away the clock pressure and what's left is the part people admire: evaluation, planning, seeing the whole board.

The orchestra. A conductor plays no instrument and makes no sound, and nobody concludes that conductors do nothing. Their job is shape, balance and timing across eighty players, which is a fair description of army command.

Real armies. Modern militaries train commanders to issue intent instead of step-by-step instruction, a doctrine called mission command with roots in the nineteenth-century Prussian army. A general who personally steers every platoon has stopped doing a general's job. The old saying that amateurs talk tactics while professionals talk logistics is, in RTS terms, a statement about macro.

Sport. Every team game has the player who is never the fastest on the field and always seems to have time. Think of the quarterback who reads the defense before the snap, or the midfielder who knows where the pass is going before the ball arrives. What looks like speed is anticipation.

What skill looks like here

None of this makes SplinterFaction easy or slow. Battles are large, there is usually more than one front, and a good opponent will hit you in two places at once. The pressure is real, and it falls on your judgment.

A strong player scouts and adapts, expands at the right moment, keeps production matched to income, and arrives at the fight with a better army in a better shape. A weak one can click as fast as they like and still lose to someone who drew one good formation and set one good repeat order.

That is the trade the game offers. Bring a plan, and the controls will carry it out. Fight smarter, not harder.
